Advocacy

BC Cycle Tourism Society works on the conditions cycle touring depends on. Corridors that get protected instead of pulled up. Trails that get repaired after a washout instead of quietly closing. Buses and ferries that will carry a bicycle.

Advocacy is one of the tools we use as a tourism economic development organization, which shapes how we work. We do the research first, publish it openly, and offer it to the people making the decision. The datasets and reports behind these positions are on our Research and Data page.

What we advocate for

Rail corridors. BC's retired rail lines are the closest thing the province has to ready-made long-distance cycling routes: gentle grades, communities every few hours, and scenery that sells itself. They are also easy to lose once a decision goes the other way. We make the case for holding corridors intact while their future is being worked out, and for trail conversion when the trains stop.

Trail designation. Surface quality is what makes a trail work for touring, and shared motorized use changes that surface quickly. We bring the touring rider's needs into designation decisions, which are usually made without anyone in the room who rides a loaded bike 80 km a day.

Climate resilience funding. Floods, fires, and washouts damage BC trails every year, and repair money is rarely part of the original build budget. A closure can outlast the damage by years. We make the case for repair funding as a standing line, and we can help estimate what a closed section costs the businesses along it.

Getting there with a bike. A trip only works if the bike can reach the start and get home from the finish. We work with ferries, buses, trains, and airports on capacity, packaging rules, and reservable bike spaces, and we publish what every carrier actually does so riders and operators are working from the same information.

Campgrounds that expect cyclists. A lower rate for arriving without a car, and always room for someone who rode in.

Sea-to-Sky rail corridor

The former BC Rail line runs about 350 km from North Vancouver through Squamish, Whistler, and the Pemberton Valley to Lillooet and on to 100 Mile House. It is one of the most scenic rail corridors in Canada. Canadian National Railway (CN) has said it intends to end its lease on the line, which put the corridor's future into play through 2026. The province has since said it intends to keep the corridor in public hands.

The conversation about the corridor's future has been mostly about freight and passenger rail. Provincial statements do not mention cycling, and regionally, trails have surfaced as one of the things people do not yet agree on. A bikeway running alongside the tracks has been raised publicly by a planner working on corridor feasibility, but as a secondary idea, without numbers attached to it.

We think rail and trail are complementary rather than competing, and there is a fair amount of published evidence for that. Around the world, a trail beside a working railway feeds the railway passengers, and the railway extends the trail's reach. The two do better together than either does alone. Our position is that a corridor that stops carrying trains should become a trail, and one that keeps carrying trains should get a bikeway alongside it wherever the terrain allows.

Through the spring of 2026 we wrote to the elected officials, planners, local governments, and study teams working on the corridor, and offered to supply that evidence. One of the feasibility study teams took us up on it, with a specific question: how much traffic could cycle tourism actually generate for a passenger service? These two reports are our answer, prepared as input to that work in April 2026.

In July 2026 we met with MLA Jeremy Valeriote and members of his staff. He is the Member of the Legislative Assembly (MLA) for West Vancouver–Sea to Sky, and his office convenes a regional roundtable on the corridor's future. We walked through the economics and talked about where cycling fits.

Rail and Trail as Complementary Infrastructure (PDF). Global evidence that rail and trail are mutually reinforcing rather than competing, with case studies from North America, Europe, Australasia, and East Asia.

Squamish–D'Arcy Bikeway: Cycle Tourism Economic Research (PDF). The economic case for a bikeway on the corridor: comparable-trail benchmarks, visitor spending evidence, First Nations connections, emergency access, and extension scenarios. There are companion appendices (PDF) with operational recommendations and full references.

All three reports are CC BY 4.0. Share them, quote them, build on them, including commercially, with credit to BC Cycle Tourism Society.

Campgrounds and no turn-away

A campground that promises to find room for someone arriving by bike, even when every site is booked, costs almost nothing and changes what is possible on a multi-day trip. In January 2026, seven cycling and outdoor recreation organizations asked Metro Vancouver Regional Parks to adopt that policy, with letters of support from four more, including Destination British Columbia and Trans Canada Trail. The letter was written by our founder, Heather Piwowar, while she was on the board of Trails BC.

The full letter, its five asks, and the places the policy already works are on our no turn-away camping page.
