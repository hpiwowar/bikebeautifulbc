BC road rules are being rewritten

July 29, 2026

You're descending into Hope on Highway 3. A truck passes at 110 km/h, close enough to feel. Whether that pass is safe, legal, or enforceable depends on BC's Motor Vehicle Act (MVA), a law written mostly with cars in mind.

That law is being rewritten. The province amended it in 2024 to require a minimum passing distance, and consultations on the next round of changes are underway now. Two BC cycling organizations, HUB Cycling and the BC Cycling Coalition, have put forward 25 recommendations for what those changes should be. Their highest-priority recommendations are strong: 30 km/h default limits on neighbourhood streets, no right turns on red, and a three-second following distance behind people on bikes.

We read all 25 with a different rider in mind. Not the commuter on Commercial Drive, but the person three days into a loaded tour, on a rural highway, crossing a rail corridor, rolling into a town they've never seen. Cycle tourists spend long days on roads where there are no protected bike lanes, only the choices made by the people driving around them. Some of the recommendations that matter most for that rider sit well down the urban priority list.

Here are our five.

Space at railway crossings#

Rail crossings can be hazardous for cyclists, especially when tracks cross the road at a shallow angle. Touring riders often need to move across the lane to line up a safer crossing, and doing that with a vehicle close behind can be one of the most stressful moments on a ride. At one rail crossing in Tennessee, redesigning the approach so cyclists crossed the rails at a steeper angle reduced observed single-bike crashes by 98 percent. Recommendation 23 would require drivers to give cyclists room to cross railway tracks and cattle guards safely.

Side-by-side riding#

Pairs and groups tour together, yet current law effectively requires single file. That makes nearly every touring group technically offside and gives impatient drivers a pretext. Recommendation 17 would make riding two abreast legal where it's safe.

Dangerous driving by large vehicles#

In the city, the answer to traffic danger is protected infrastructure. On a rural highway there usually isn't any. Safety depends on whether the driver of the truck gives you room. Recommendation 13 would lower the threshold for licence suspension, raise fines for the largest vehicles, and make retraining routine after repeat offences.

Stop signs as yields#

On a multi-day ride you stop at a lot of empty rural intersections. The "Idaho stop" lets cyclists treat a stop sign as a yield: slow, look, proceed if clear. Idaho's cyclist injuries dropped 14.5 percent in the year after the law took effect, and HUB Cycling already advocates for it here.

Safer highway speeds#

The speed gap between a loaded bike and highway traffic is largest exactly where cycle tourists ride. Recommendation 10 would expand GPS-aware speed limiters, technology the European Union already requires in new vehicles.

The full list, with our take on all 25, is in the appendix at the bottom of this page.

None of this replaces the urban priorities. It adds another lens: visitors on unfamiliar roads, loaded bikes, rail corridors, and town connectors. The laws governing BC's roads matter just as much as the routes themselves.

Have your say#

The BC Cycling Coalition is surveying riders to shape what goes to the province, and they'll be sharing the results with government. Membership is free and takes a minute. Take the survey. If you ride BC's roads, your perspective is exactly what's missing from this conversation.

— Heather

Appendix: all 25 recommendations, through a cycle tourist's lens#

The full list of HUB Cycling and the BC Cycling Coalition's 2024 Motor Vehicle Act reform recommendations, presented in their original order, with our take on what each one means for people touring BC by bike.

# Recommendation Our take
1Default 30 km/h limit on local streets without centre linesModerate. Every tour passes through towns, and slower streets make unfamiliar ones gentler. Mostly a residential-street change.
2No right turns on red for motor vehiclesModerate-high. Removes a common conflict point at the intersections you meet entering and leaving town.
3Welcome wheelchairs and mobility scooters on cycling routesLow for touring specifically, though it makes shared paths more welcoming for everyone.
4Allow small children on sidewalks at walking speedMinimal for touring. Matters for families, not multi-day trips.
5Three-second following distance behind vulnerable road usersHigh. Being followed too closely by fast traffic is a familiar experience on rural highways. An enforceable buffer matters most where there is no bike lane to retreat to.
6Fines for blocking bike lanes, with automated enforcementModerate. Mostly protects urban bike lanes. Relevant when your route crosses a city.
7Let cyclists use the pedestrian head-start signal at intersectionsModerate. Simpler intersections help riders who don't know them.
8Remove the mandatory adult helmet lawMainly affects spontaneous and urban visitor cycling, especially bike share. Touring riders generally bring helmets, so the impact on multi-day trips is small.
9Make "elephant's feet" crossings a provincial standardLow. A consistency fix with a small benefit at town crossings.
10Expand GPS-aware speed limitersHigh. The speed gap between bikes and traffic is biggest on the rural highways where tours happen. Anything that brings vehicle speeds down there cuts the worst risk.
11Stop sign as yield for cyclists (the Idaho stop)High. Touring means hundreds of rural stop signs. Slowing and proceeding when it's clearly empty beats coming to a complete stop at every one. HUB Cycling already advocates for this.
12Allow passing on the right on shoulders and bikewaysLow. A technical legal fix, rarely an issue on touring routes.
13Stronger penalties and retraining for dangerous driving, higher fines for the largest vehiclesHigh. On a rural highway there is no protected lane, just the driver's choices. Heavy vehicles pose one of the greatest risks to touring cyclists on rural highways.
14Allow bicycles in rightmost bus lanesLow. Urban only.
15Modernize cyclist signalling rulesLow. A clarification, though a welcome one on a loaded bike when taking a hand off the bars is unsafe.
16Clarify right-of-way at roundabouts and traffic circlesHigh. Roundabouts are increasingly common in resort towns, and visitors often aren't sure who yields to whom. Clear rules help everyone.
17Allow side-by-side riding where safeHigh. Pairs and groups tour together, and the single-file rule makes that technically illegal, which some drivers treat as an invitation.
18Require side guards on heavy trucksHigh. Side guards reduce the risk of cyclists being pulled beneath turning trucks. Particularly relevant where touring cyclists and heavy vehicles share the same road.
19Drivers must yield to a cyclist continuing through an intersectionHigh. Getting cut off by turning vehicles is a constant hazard when you're following a route through unfamiliar intersections.
20Tighten safe passing to 1.5 m at speeds under 50 km/hModerate. Since 2024, drivers are required to give you 1.5 m at highway speeds but only 1 m in town. This would make the full 1.5 m the rule everywhere.
21Clarify that cyclists ride as near to the right as is safe, not as far right as possibleHigh. Loaded bikes need room to avoid debris, grates, and rough shoulders. Legal clarity protects riders who are already doing the right thing.
22No duty to pull right just because a driver honksModerate. A horn should not be able to legally push you into the gravel.
23Drivers must give cyclists space to cross railway tracks and cattle guardsVery high. Touring riders sometimes need to move across the lane to line up a safer crossing. Having room to do that matters. Especially relevant on rural roads with rail crossings or cattle guards.
24Rename the Act to the "Road Safety Act"Symbolic. No practical effect on a ride.
25Let cyclists proceed past the stop line to trigger a signalLow, but real: plenty of signals never detect a bicycle.

Sources: Modernizing the BC Motor Vehicle Act, HUB Cycling and BC Cycling Coalition (2024); rail crossing research, University of Tennessee; stop-as-yield fact sheet, NHTSA.
